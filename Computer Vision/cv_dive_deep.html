Overview

Computer vision encompasses algorithms, architectures, and pipelines designed to extract high-level semantic understanding, geometric structure, and generative control from digital images and videos. This document establishes an end-to-end technical reference spanning classical feature engineering, dense spatial segmentation, state-of-the-art transformer backbones, attention visualization, and multimodal generative architectures.

The field has evolved from hand-crafted filters, corners, edges, and local descriptors into learned convolutional, transformer-based, generative, and multimodal systems. Classical methods remain valuable because they are interpretable, data-efficient, and useful for geometric reasoning. Deep models dominate modern perception because they learn hierarchical representations directly from data.

Part 1: Classical Computer Vision & Feature Engineering

Computer vision hierarchy

Processing pipelines in computer vision are traditionally categorized into three hierarchical levels:

  • Low-level vision (image-to-image): Operations such as filtering, blurring, sharpening, edge extraction, and optical-flow estimation that transform or analyze primitive pixel data.
  • Intermediate-level vision (image-to-features): Extraction of symbolic features, contours, regions, and local descriptors from preprocessed image patches to support object recognition and 3D interpretation.
  • High-level vision (features-to-analysis): Semantic clustering, classification, activity recognition, scene understanding, and intent interpretation.

Local feature detectors and descriptors

Local features provide compact representations that can be matched across changes in viewpoint, scale, rotation, and illumination. A local feature pipeline generally has two components:

  1. Detector: finds repeatable image locations, such as corners, blobs, or extrema.
  2. Descriptor: converts the local neighborhood into a vector or binary signature for matching.

Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT)

Scale-Invariant Feature Transform, or SIFT, detects scale-space extrema using a Difference of Gaussians (DoG) pyramid. Keypoints are filtered to remove low-contrast responses and edge artifacts. Descriptors are built from gradient orientation histograms over local sub-regions, producing a 128-dimensional vector. ai.googleblog

SIFT is robust to scale and rotation, but it is computationally more expensive than modern binary-feature methods.

Affine SIFT (ASIFT)

Affine SIFT, or ASIFT, extends SIFT by simulating a comprehensive set of camera-axis orientation parameters—commonly described as latitude and longitude—before applying standard SIFT. This provides stronger invariance to affine viewpoint changes than SIFT alone.

ASIFT is useful when the camera viewpoint changes substantially, but it multiplies the number of images that must be processed and therefore increases computation.

Speeded-Up Robust Features (SURF)

Speeded-Up Robust Features, or SURF, approximates Hessian-matrix determinants using box filters evaluated rapidly through integral images, also called summed-area tables. SURF produces 64-dimensional descriptors and was designed to be faster than SIFT while retaining reasonable robustness.

Oriented FAST and Rotated BRIEF (ORB)

Oriented FAST and Rotated BRIEF, or ORB, combines the FAST keypoint detector with a BRIEF binary descriptor. It adds multi-scale image pyramids and orientation assignment to improve rotation invariance. ORB is widely used in real-time systems because binary descriptors are efficient to compute and match.

Edge detection and image enhancement

Canny edge detection

Canny edge detection is a multi-stage edge detector:

  1. Apply Gaussian smoothing to suppress noise.
  2. Compute image gradients, commonly with Sobel operators.
  3. Apply non-maximum suppression along gradient directions.
  4. Apply hysteresis thresholding using high and low thresholds to connect edge segments.
\[G_x = \frac{\partial I}{\partial x}, \qquad G_y = \frac{\partial I}{\partial y}\] \[G = \sqrt{G_x^2 + G_y^2}, \qquad \theta = \arctan\left(\frac{G_y}{G_x}\right)\]

where:

  • $I$ is the input image.
  • $G_x$ and $G_y$ are horizontal and vertical gradients.
  • $G$ is gradient magnitude.
  • $\theta$ is gradient direction.

Non-maximum suppression keeps only local maxima along the gradient direction. Hysteresis thresholding connects weak edges to strong edges, reducing fragmented edge maps.

Harris corner detector

The Harris corner detector identifies points where intensity changes significantly in multiple directions. It computes a structure tensor from local image gradients:

\[M = \sum_{x,y} w(x,y) \begin{bmatrix} I_x^2 & I_x I_y \\ I_x I_y & I_y^2 \end{bmatrix}\]

where:

  • $I_x$ and $I_y$ are image gradients.
  • $w(x,y)$ is a windowing function, often Gaussian.

The Harris response is commonly computed as:

\[R = \det(M) - k \cdot \text{trace}(M)^2\]

where $k$ is a sensitivity parameter, often around 0.04 to 0.06.

Corners produce large positive Harris responses because the eigenvalues of $M$ are both large. Edges produce one large and one small eigenvalue. Flat regions produce two small eigenvalues.

Structural Similarity Index (SSIM)

Structural Similarity Index, or SSIM, evaluates perceived structural degradation between two images by comparing luminance, contrast, and structure. SSIM values fall within $[-1, 1]$, where $1$ denotes identical structure.

\[\text{SSIM}(x,y) = \frac{ (2\mu_x\mu_y + C_1)(2\sigma_{xy} + C_2) }{ (\mu_x^2 + \mu_y^2 + C_1)(\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2 + C_2) }\]

where:

  • $x$ and $y$ are image patches.
  • $\mu_x$ and $\mu_y$ are local means.
  • $\sigma_x^2$ and $\sigma_y^2$ are local variances.
  • $\sigma_{xy}$ is local covariance.
  • $C_1$ and $C_2$ stabilize division.

SSIM is generally more perceptually meaningful than mean squared error (MSE), although neither metric perfectly captures human perception.

Geometric alignment

Homography

A homography relates two views of the same planar surface in 3D space. It is a $3 \times 3$ projective transformation:

\[\begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \sim H \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\]

where:

  • $(x, y)$ is a point in the first image.
  • $(x’, y’)$ is the corresponding point in the second image.
  • $H$ is a $3 \times 3$ homography matrix.
  • $\sim$ denotes equality up to scale.

A homography has eight degrees of freedom after scale normalization. At least four non-collinear point correspondences are required to estimate it.

RANSAC

Random Sample Consensus, or RANSAC, estimates geometric models while rejecting outliers.

Repeat:
1. Randomly select minimum number of correspondences
2. Estimate homography
3. Count inliers within error threshold
4. Keep model with most inliers
5. Refit model using all inliers

RANSAC is robust to incorrect feature matches, but it is stochastic and may require many iterations when the outlier ratio is high.

Part 2: Dense Prediction, Object Detection, and Segmentation

Object detection fundamentals

Object detection answers two questions:

  1. What objects are present?
  2. Where are they located?

Traditional detectors often use sliding windows, hand-crafted features, and classifiers. Modern detectors use convolutional or transformer backbones, learned region proposals or object queries, and differentiable losses.

Focal loss and RetinaNet

Single-stage detectors evaluate dense grids of candidate anchor boxes but suffer from extreme class imbalance during training, where background regions dominate foreground objects. Focal loss dynamically scales cross-entropy loss based on prediction confidence to down-weight easy background examples. ai.googleblog

Given the model’s estimated probability $p_t$ for the ground-truth class, focal loss is:

\[\text{FL}(p_t) = -\alpha_t(1 - p_t)^\gamma \log(p_t)\]

where:

  • $p_t$ is the predicted probability for the ground-truth class.
  • $\alpha_t$ balances class frequencies.
  • $\gamma$ is the focusing parameter.
  • Larger $\gamma$ reduces the contribution of easy examples.

RetinaNet uses $\alpha = 0.25$ and $\gamma = 2$ in its original formulation. ai.googleblog

Easy background example:
p_t close to 1
(1 - p_t)^gamma close to 0
Loss contribution small

Hard foreground example:
p_t low
(1 - p_t)^gamma close to 1
Loss contribution large

Part 3: Vision Transformers and Modern Architectures

Vision Transformer (ViT)

Vision Transformers adapt the standard natural-language-processing transformer encoder to image recognition by processing non-overlapping flattened image patches. ai.googleblog

Input image
  |
  +-- Split into patches --> [Patch 1, Patch 2, ..., Patch N]
  |
  +-- Linear projection --> Patch embeddings + learnable [class] token
  |
  +-- Position encoding --> Add spatial information
  |
  +-- Transformer encoder --> Multi-head self-attention + MLP blocks
  |
  +-- Classification head --> Read [class] token output

For an image of size $H \times W \times C$, patch size $P$, and embedding dimension $D$, the number of patches is:

\[N = \frac{H}{P} \times \frac{W}{P}\]

Each patch is flattened and linearly projected into a $D$-dimensional token embedding.

Self-attention computes relationships among all patch tokens:

\[\text{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V\]

where:

  • $Q$, $K$, and $V$ are query, key, and value matrices.
  • $d_k$ is the key dimension.

The main trade-off is that standard self-attention has quadratic complexity in the number of tokens:

\[O(N^2)\]

This becomes expensive for high-resolution images or small patch sizes.

Detection Transformer (DETR)

DETR casts object detection directly as a set-prediction problem, eliminating hand-crafted components such as anchor generation and non-maximum suppression. It uses a convolutional backbone, a transformer encoder-decoder, and a fixed set of $N$ learned object queries. ai.googleblog

Transformer decoder
     |
     +-- Object query 1 --> box + class
     +-- Object query 2 --> box + class

DETR uses bipartite matching with the Hungarian algorithm to assign each prediction to at most one ground-truth object. This removes duplicate detections and avoids non-maximum suppression.

DeNoising DETR (DINO)

DeNoising DETR, or DINO, improves DETR-style detectors by introducing denoising training. During training, the model receives noised ground-truth boxes and learns to reconstruct the original boxes, improving query initialization and convergence.

Part 4: Generative Computer Vision Models

Autoencoders

An autoencoder learns a compressed latent representation of an input and reconstructs the input from that representation.

Input image x
     |
     v
Encoder E(x)
     |
     v
Latent code z
     |
     v
Decoder D(z)
     |
     v
Reconstruction x_hat

The reconstruction objective is commonly:

\[\mathcal{L}_{\text{recon}} = \|x - D(E(x))\|^2\]

Autoencoders are useful for denoising, compression, anomaly detection, and representation learning. A standard autoencoder does not necessarily learn a well-structured generative latent space.

Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)

A Variational Autoencoder parameterizes an approximate latent distribution $q_\phi(z \mid x)$ by predicting a mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$. To enable backpropagation through stochastic sampling, it uses the reparameterization trick:

\[z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\]

where:

  • $z$ is the latent variable.
  • $\mu$ is the predicted mean.
  • $\sigma$ is the predicted standard deviation.
  • $\epsilon$ is sampled noise.
  • $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication.

The VAE optimizes the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO):

\[\mathcal{L}_{\text{VAE}} = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid x)} \left[ -\log p_\theta(x \mid z) \right] + D_{\text{KL}} \left( q_\phi(z \mid x) \parallel p(z) \right)\]

where:

  • $p_\theta(x \mid z)$ is the decoder likelihood.
  • $p(z)$ is the prior, commonly $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$.
  • $D_{\text{KL}}$ is the Kullback-Leibler divergence.

The first term encourages accurate reconstruction. The KL term regularizes the latent distribution toward the prior.

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)

A Generative Adversarial Network trains two networks in competition:

  • A generator $G$ maps noise $z$ to an image.
  • A discriminator $D$ distinguishes real images from generated images.
Noise z
   |
   v
Generator G
   |
   v
Fake image
   |
   +--> Discriminator D --> real or fake
   |
Real image ------------------^

The original minimax objective is:

\[\min_G \max_D \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}} [\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z} [\log(1 - D(G(z)))]\]

where:

  • $x$ is a real image.
  • $z$ is a latent noise vector.
  • $G(z)$ is a generated image.
  • $D(x)$ is the discriminator’s probability that $x$ is real.

GANs can produce sharp images but are notoriously unstable. Common problems include mode collapse, non-convergence, vanishing gradients, and sensitivity to hyperparameters.

Diffusion models

Diffusion models generate data by learning to reverse a fixed Markov chain of forward Gaussian noising steps. Starting from a data sample $x_0$, noise is progressively added across $T$ timesteps to produce $x_1, \ldots, x_T$. ai.googleblog

Forward diffusion:
x_0 -> x_1 -> x_2 -> ... -> x_T

Reverse denoising:
x_T -> x_T-1 -> ... -> x_1 -> x_0

The forward process is commonly:

\[q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N} \left( x_t; \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}x_{t-1}, \beta_t I \right)\]

where:

  • $\beta_t$ is a variance schedule.
  • $x_t$ is the noisy sample at timestep $t$.

The model learns the reverse process:

\[p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)\]

typically by predicting the noise added at each step.

Diffusion models generally produce high-quality samples and train more stably than GANs, but they require multiple denoising steps at inference, increasing latency.

MaskGIT is a masked generative image transformer. It first encodes an image into discrete visual tokens, then generates an image by iteratively predicting and unmasking tokens.

Part 5: Attention Mechanisms and Explainability

Channel and spatial attention

Attention modules help convolutional networks emphasize informative channels and spatial locations.

Bottleneck Attention Module (BAM)

The Bottleneck Attention Module computes attention at network bottlenecks using parallel channel and spatial sub-modules. It suppresses irrelevant low-level background features and emphasizes task-relevant information.

Grad-CAM

Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping, or Grad-CAM, produces coarse localization heatmaps by computing gradients of a target class score $y^c$ with respect to feature maps $A^k$ from a chosen convolutional layer. ai.googleblog

The importance weight for feature map $k$ is:

\[\alpha_k^c = \frac{1}{Z} \sum_i \sum_j \frac{\partial y^c}{\partial A_{i,j}^k}\]

where:

  • $y^c$ is the score for class $c$.
  • $A_{i,j}^k$ is activation at spatial position $(i,j)$ in feature map $k$.
  • $Z$ is the number of spatial positions.

The final heatmap is:

\[L_{\text{Grad-CAM}}^c = \text{ReLU} \left( \sum_k \alpha_k^c A^k \right)\]

ReLU retains only features with a positive influence on the target class.

Input image
     |
     v

     |
     v
Target-class gradients
     |
     v
Global-average-pooled weights
     |
     v
Weighted feature-map combination
     |
     v
ReLU
     |
     v

Grad-CAM is useful for debugging, but it is an explanation method, not proof of causality.

Saliency maps

Saliency maps highlight input pixels that most influence a model’s prediction. A simple saliency method computes the gradient of the class score with respect to the input image:

\[S = \left| \frac{\partial y^c}{\partial x} \right|\]

where:

  • $y^c$ is the score for class $c$.
  • $x$ is the input image.
  • $S$ is the saliency map.

Large gradient magnitudes indicate input locations where a small change would most affect the class score.

Part 6: Multimodal Learning

Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training (CLIP)

Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training, or CLIP, jointly trains an image encoder and a text encoder on web-scale image-caption pairs using a symmetric contrastive loss. It maximizes cosine similarity between matching image-text pairs while minimizing similarity across incorrect pairings in the batch. ai.googleblog

Image batch:
[I1, I2, ..., IN]

Text batch:
[T1, T2, ..., TN]

Similarity matrix:
        T1   T2   T3
I1   [ high  low   low ]
I2   [ low   high  low ]
I3   [ low   low   high]

For a batch of $N$ image-text pairs, the image-to-text contrastive loss is:

\[\mathcal{L}_{I \rightarrow T} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log \frac{ \exp(\text{sim}(I_i, T_i)/\tau) }{ \sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp(\text{sim}(I_i, T_j)/\tau) }\]

where:

  • $I_i$ is an image embedding.
  • $T_j$ is a text embedding.
  • $\text{sim}$ is cosine similarity.
  • $\tau$ is a learned temperature parameter.

CLIP enables zero-shot classification by comparing an image embedding with text embeddings for candidate labels.

BLIP

Bootstrapping Language-Image Pre-training, or BLIP, extends CLIP’s dual-encoder alignment with a unified encoder-decoder mixture architecture. It supports both multimodal understanding tasks, such as retrieval and classification, and generative tasks, such as captioning and visual question answering. ai.googleblog

Image + text
     |
     v
Shared multimodal backbone
     |
     +--> Image-text contrastive learning
     |
     +--> Image-text matching
     |
     +--> Language modeling / captioning

BLIP also introduces synthetic caption bootstrapping: it generates captions for web images, filters noisy text, and uses the improved data to enhance pre-training.

Segment Anything Model (SAM)

The Segment Anything Model, or SAM, is a promptable segmentation foundation model. It can produce segmentation masks from prompts such as points, boxes, or text, depending on the deployment configuration.

Part 8: Limitations and Open Questions

Computational complexity

Attention-based mechanisms such as standard Vision Transformers and dense transformer decoders incur quadratic complexity relative to spatial resolution or token count:

\[O(N^2)\]

where $N$ is the number of tokens. This makes high-resolution images, video, and dense prediction expensive.

Small object detection

Global reasoning models can struggle with small objects that occupy only a few pixels. Feature pyramid networks, high-resolution feature maps, multi-scale heads, and local attention mechanisms can help.

Generative model limitations

Model family Main limitation
GANs Mode collapse, unstable training, convergence failures
VAEs Blurry reconstructions and weaker sample quality
Diffusion models Multi-step sampling increases inference latency
Autoregressive models Sequential token generation is slow
Masked generative models Requires discrete tokenization and iterative decoding

Interpretability limitations

Grad-CAM and saliency maps are useful diagnostic tools, but they do not prove causal explanations. They can highlight correlations, be sensitive to architecture and input perturbations, and fail to capture higher-level reasoning.

Data and bias

Vision models inherit biases from training data. Multimodal models can associate demographic attributes, objects, scenes, and captions in ways that produce unfair or unsafe behavior. Evaluation must include demographic slices, geographic coverage, adversarial cases, and failure analysis.

Final Technical Takeaways

  • Hierarchical progression: Low-level operators such as Canny, Harris, SIFT, and ORB provide geometric foundations, while convolutional and transformer backbones deliver high-level semantic abstraction.
  • Feature matching: SIFT, ASIFT, SURF, and ORB remain useful for alignment, retrieval, and geometric vision, especially when labeled data is limited.
  • Hard constraints matter: Focal loss addresses extreme foreground-background imbalance in dense object detection.
  • End-to-end set prediction: DETR, DINO, and MaX-DeepLab reduce reliance on anchors, boxes, and non-maximum suppression by using learned queries, bipartite matching, and mask transformers. ai.googleblog
  • Attention improves selectivity: BAM and CBAM refine features by emphasizing important channels and spatial locations.
  • Explainability is approximate: Grad-CAM and saliency maps provide useful diagnostics but are not causal proof.
  • Multimodal alignment: CLIP learns a shared vision-language embedding space, while BLIP extends this toward captioning and visual question answering.
  • Main trade-off: Transformers and diffusion models provide strong capability but require more compute, memory, and inference time than classical or convolutional alternatives.
  • Broader implication: Modern computer vision is a layered system: geometric foundations, learned perception, generative modeling, multimodal alignment, and explainability must be designed together.
